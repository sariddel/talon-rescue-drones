The gap

Between the distress call and the first responder on scene, the search area only grows.

When a fishing vessel capsizes 70 nautical miles offshore, the nearest cutter is three or four hours away at 20 knots. Wind and current push the survivors further from their last known position every minute. By the time a vessel arrives, the place to look has grown from a point to hundreds of square kilometres — and a person in cold water does not have hours.

The system

A drone in a tube. A search that starts before anyone arrives.

TALON is a sealed, cylindrical cartridge that lives on a vessel's deck or in a shore station's rack — no airframe to assemble, no runway, no launch crew. Pull the pin, and an autonomous search aircraft is over the water in minutes, streaming thermal and electro-optical video back to the operator.

Tube-launched in minutes

A compact cartridge deploys without assembly or a launch crew. From a cutter, a corvette, or a shoreline rack — the same tube, the same four-minute sequence.

Thermal & EO sensors

A gimballed thermal imager picks human body heat off cold water day or night; an electro-optical camera confirms and reads survival suits, hulls, and debris. The drone searches; the operator decides.

Search theory, automated

Each drone flies an optimal sweep pattern over the drift-adjusted datum. Coverage and probability of detection are computed live, so the operator fields exactly the swarm the area demands — no more, no less.

Drops a GPS marker

On a confirmed sighting the drone marks the position and loiters overhead, turning the inbound helicopter's job from an open-ocean search into a direct recovery.

Cheaper than getting underway

A swarm of expendable drones costs a fraction of a vessel sortie or a helicopter launch — even if some are never recovered. The math favours putting eyes on the water first.

One operator, many drones

A single watchstander manages the swarm from the existing bridge console. The system does the flying and the searching; the human keeps command of the picture.

Two missions, one tube

Extend what a ship can find — or what it can see coming.

The same launch infrastructure serves a humanitarian search and a military picture. The drone that finds a survivor in the dark is the drone that builds a track on a contact over the horizon.

Coast Guard · SAR

Extend the search range

A cutter searches the water it can physically reach. TALON pushes that reach out to the datum the moment the Mayday lands — fanning a swarm across the drift circle and getting a sighting before the hull is even underway.

  • Eyes on the last-known-position in minutes, not hours
  • Thermal detection of survivors in cold water, day or night
  • GPS-marked datum hands the helicopter a fix, not a search box
  • Persisting coverage as the drift area grows
Navy · ISR

Extend the awareness range

A warship's horizon is a hard limit on warning time. TALON turns the same deck tube into a persistent over-horizon sensor — pushing the detection bubble outward so a surface contact is tracked long before it closes to weapons range.

  • Over-the-horizon surveillance from existing launch hardware
  • Earlier track on surface and small-craft contacts
  • Distributed sensing without risking the ship's position
  • Low-cost, attritable, and rapidly re-fielded

A morning in the Bering Sea

The crew made it home because the search began before anyone arrived.

The Bering Sea is the most dangerous workplace in North America. The Coast Guard's 17th District patrols 3.5 million square kilometres of remote, storm-prone ocean — and the nearest cutter to most active fishing grounds sits 60–90 nautical miles away. This gap between distress and arrival is where people die.

T+0 · MAYDAY

A crabber capsizes in the Pribilof Canyon

Three crew are in near-freezing water. The nearest cutter is 74 nautical miles out — over three and a half hours away. The EPIRB gives a last-known position, but current and drift mean the search area grows with every passing minute.

T+4 MIN · AIRBORNE

Tubes fire from the cutter and a shore station

Four minutes after the Mayday, TALON drones are over the water — climbing out toward the datum while the cutter is still building speed.

T+51 MIN · ON SCENE

The swarm reaches the datum — three hours ahead of the hull

Fanning out across the drift circle, the drones locate all three survivors within thirty minutes and drop GPS markers, turning the inbound helicopter's mission from a search into a direct rescue.

T<2 HRS · RECOVERED

Last survivor aboard the helicopter

Total time from Mayday to last survivor recovered: under two hours — against a vessel-only timeline that would not have put a single set of eyes on scene for the better part of an hour.
